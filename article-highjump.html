跳高選手的腰痛之謎
— 從腰方肌到骨盆穩定性的完整案例

腰痛反覆出現，不是因為腰方肌需要更多放鬆，而是因為骨盆與髖關節的動作控制出現了問題。

The High Jumper's Lower Back Pain Mystery
— From Quadratus Lumborum to Pelvic Stability

Recurring lower back pain isn't solved by more muscle release — it requires rebuilding pelvic and hip movement control from the ground up.

by Coach Hill

引子

Edison 是一位跳高選手兼教練。

右腰痛已經困擾他一段時間。之前有過急性拉傷，治療師放鬆過腰方肌，但疼痛卻始終沒有完全消失。

他來找我時問了一個關鍵問題：「為什麼上次放鬆後還是會痛？」

為什麼只放鬆腰方肌不夠？

這是一個很常見的誤解：腰痛 = 腰肌緊張 = 放鬆腰肌。

看起來邏輯簡單，但實際上忽略了一個重點：肌肉之所以會緊張，通常是因為它在過度代償。

如果你不解決代償的根源，只放鬆肌肉，痛點就會反覆出現。

Edison 的真正問題在哪裡？

跳高是一項高度不對稱的運動。每次起跳時，Edison 的右腳做出大幅髖屈動作（open-chain position），這表示他的骨盆必須能夠穩定地承受這個動作。

長期下來，這種單側、高負荷的動作模式開始「崩壞」。

我們用 CAK 臨床肌動學重新評估，問了三個核心問題：

  1. 骨盆在髖屈時能穩定嗎？ — 骨盆會不會傾斜或旋轉？
  2. 髖部肌肉有沒有均衡發力？ — 還是某些肌肉過度活躍，其他肌肉睡著了？
  3. 核心能否動態支撐？ — 當單邊做動作時，對側肌肉有沒有跟上？

檢測結果

CAK 評估發現

同側腰方肌（Right QL）張力異常偏高 ↑
對側腹外斜肌（Left external oblique）張力異常偏高 ↑
同側腹內斜肌（Right internal oblique）張力異常偏高 ↑
核心動作控制無法順利執行 Hip Hike（骨盆提起）動作

為什麼會出現這個模式？

跳高時，Edison 的右腳做髖屈，左側骨盆傾向於下沉。為了防止骨盆完全傾斜，他的身體自動啟動了多條穩定肌：

  • 右腰方肌拚命上拉骨盆 → 過度活躍 → 容易疲勞 → 疼痛
  • 左側腹外斜肌也加入支撐 → 但發力不當 → 創造了額外的代償
  • 右側腹內斜肌同時被拉扯 → 也跟著收緊

結果：整個腰椎段被過度牽引，加上之前的急性拉傷留下的疤痕組織，疼痛就成了反覆的問題。

為什麼光放鬆不行？

單純放鬆這些緊張的肌肉就像「拆除煙霧偵測器」而不是「滅火」。如果 Edison 不重建他的骨盆穩定性，腰肌就會一次又一次地被迫過度代償。

訓練策略

第 1 階段：啟動被「遺忘」的肌肉

  • 對側臀中肌激活 — 側向行走、側向跨步，穩定左側骨盆
  • 對側腹內斜肌喚醒 — 側棒式變化、轉身動作
  • 髖屈肌靈活性 — 低位弓步伸展，為髖屈活動度預留空間

第 2 階段：重建 Hip Hike（骨盆提起）控制

  • 坐姿 Hip Hike（輔助理解動作模式）
  • 站姿扶牆 Hip Hike（建立本體感覺）
  • 站姿開放 Hip Hike（獨立控制）
  • 行走中的 Hip Hike（動態整合）

第 3 階段：動態穩定性訓練

  • 單腳站立轉身 — 模擬跳高的不對稱動作
  • 單腳蹲 — 在不穩定時維持骨盆穩定
  • 開放鏈髖屈 — 在跳高的實際位置練習穩定性

第 4 階段：漸進返回專項訓練

  • 逐步增加負荷和動作複雜度
  • 在跳高動作中監測骨盆穩定性
  • 建立賽前熱身中的骨盆啟動習慣

結果

經過放鬆加訓練，Edison 的腰痛大幅改善。更重要的是：

  • 他現在知道為什麼腰會痛
  • 他能夠自我檢測骨盆穩定性
  • 他有工具可以預防疼痛復發
  • 他的跳高表現反而進步了

你的腰痛也是「放鬆就會好」嗎？

如果你的腰痛反覆發生，只放鬆肌肉卻沒有改善根本的動作控制問題，那麼問題可能和 Edison 一樣 — 骨盆穩定性丟失。

特別是如果你的運動項目涉及單側動作（跳高、跨欄、單腿起跳等），你更需要檢視的不是「哪條肌肉太緊」，而是「哪一側的穩定肌在偷懶」。

你的腰痛也反覆出現嗎？

透過骨盆穩定性評估，找出你的代償根源，從根本解決問題。

預約骨盆穩定性評估

免責聲明：以上內容為調整訓練建議，非醫療診斷或治療。本案例未使用任何治療性質的手法。

Opening

Edison is a high jumper and coach.

Right-sided lower back pain has been bothering him. He'd experienced an acute strain previously, and a therapist released his quadratus lumborum, but the pain never fully resolved.

When he came in, he asked a critical question: "Why does the pain keep coming back after the release?"

Why Releasing the Quadratus Lumborum Isn't Enough

This is a common misconception: Lower back pain = tight back muscles = release muscles.

The logic seems straightforward, but it misses a crucial point: Muscles become tight because they're over-compensating.

If you don't address the source of compensation, simply releasing muscles will lead to repeated pain cycles.

Where Was Edison's Real Problem?

High jumping is a highly asymmetrical sport. Every takeoff involves Edison's right leg performing a significant hip flexion movement in an open-chain position — meaning his pelvis must be capable of stable control throughout.

Over time, this unilateral, high-load movement pattern began to "break down."

We reassessed using CAK clinical kinesiology, asking three core questions:

  1. Can the pelvis stay stable during hip flexion? — Does it tilt or rotate?
  2. Are hip muscles balanced? — Or are some over-dominant while others dormant?
  3. Can the core provide dynamic support? — When one leg moves, does the opposite side stabilize?

Assessment Findings

CAK Assessment Results

Ipsilateral quadratus lumborum (Right QL)Hypertonicity ↑
Contralateral external oblique (Left)Hypertonicity ↑
Ipsilateral internal oblique (Right)Hypertonicity ↑
Movement control deficitUnable to perform Hip Hike smoothly

Why This Compensation Pattern Developed

During high jump takeoff, Edison's right hip flexes while his left pelvis tends to drop. To prevent complete pelvic tilt, his body automatically engaged multiple stabilizers:

  • Right QL pulled the pelvis up forcefully → Over-activation → Fatigue → Pain
  • Left external oblique also engaged → Improper force distribution → Additional compensation
  • Right internal oblique simultaneously loaded → Also tightened

Result: The lumbar spine experienced excessive traction, combined with scarring from the previous acute strain, creating a cycle of recurring pain.

Why Pure Release Isn't Sufficient

Only relaxing these tight muscles is like "removing the smoke detector" instead of "putting out the fire." If Edison doesn't rebuild pelvic stability, his back muscles will repeatedly be forced to over-compensate.

Our Training Strategy

Phase 1: Activate "Forgotten" Muscles

  • Contralateral glute medius activation — sidestepping, lateral lunges to stabilize left pelvis
  • Contralateral internal oblique engagement — side plank variations, rotation movements
  • Hip flexor mobility — low lunge stretches to preserve hip flexion range

Phase 2: Rebuild Hip Hike Control

  • Seated Hip Hike (assisted comprehension of the movement)
  • Wall-supported standing Hip Hike (proprioceptive feedback)
  • Open standing Hip Hike (independent control)
  • Gait-integrated Hip Hike (dynamic integration)

Phase 3: Dynamic Stability Training

  • Single-leg stance with rotation — mimicking high jump asymmetry
  • Single-leg squat — maintaining pelvic stability under load
  • Open-chain hip flexion — stability in the actual high jump position

Phase 4: Progressive Return to Sport

  • Gradually increase load and movement complexity
  • Monitor pelvic stability during jump-specific movements
  • Build pre-competition pelvic activation warm-up habits

Results

After release and training, Edison's lower back pain significantly improved. More importantly:

  • He now understands why his back hurts
  • He can self-assess pelvic stability
  • He has tools to prevent pain recurrence
  • His high jump performance actually improved

Is Your Lower Back Pain Also "Just a Release Away"?

If your back pain recurs and releasing muscles doesn't address the root movement control issue, your problem may be like Edison's — lost pelvic stability.

Especially if your sport involves unilateral movements (high jump, hurdles, single-leg takeoff), you need to investigate not "which muscle is too tight," but rather "which stabilizer is neglecting its job."
